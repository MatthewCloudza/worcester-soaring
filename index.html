Learn: Reading the Forecast

What is CAPE?

CAPE (Convective Available Potential Energy) measures how much energy is available for thermals. Think of it as "thermal fuel".

Higher CAPE = stronger thermals that go higher.

0-100 J/kg = No useful thermals (stay home)
100-500 = Weak thermals (local flying only)
500-1000 = Moderate (good soaring day)
1000+ = Strong (XC potential, but watch for overdevelopment)

At Worcester, a "good" winter day might only show 200-400 J/kg. In summer, 800+ is common.

What is the Showalter Index?

Showalter Index measures atmospheric stability. It's the temperature difference between a lifted parcel and its environment at 500 hPa.

Negative = unstable = good for thermals. Positive = stable = thermals capped.

Below 0 = Unstable (thermals likely)
0 to 3 = Marginal (weak thermals possible)
3 to 6 = Stable (thermals unlikely)
Above 6 = Very stable (no chance)

At Worcester, anything below 3 in winter is actually worth looking at.

What is the LCL (Cloudbase)?

LCL (Lifted Condensation Level) is where clouds form. For pilots, this is your expected cloudbase.

Below 3000 ft AGL = Low cloudbase (limited height)
3000-5000 ft = Moderate (decent local soaring)
5000-8000 ft = Good (XC conditions)
Above 8000 ft = High but may be blue (no cu)

Very high LCL (7000+ ft) can mean "blue" thermals with no cloud marking.

Lapse Rate & Inversions

Lapse Rate is how quickly temperature drops with altitude. Faster cooling = more unstable = better thermals.

Stable = Thermals die quickly
Marginal = Weak, broken thermals
Unstable = Strong, continuous thermals

Inversions are layers where temperature increases with height. Thermals cannot punch through them. They act like a lid on your altitude.

Ridge Soaring at Worcester

Ridge lift forms when wind hits a mountain slope and deflects upward. No thermals needed.

Slanghoek: Needs NW wind (15+ kt at 850 hPa)
Du Toitskloof: Needs W to NW wind
Hex River: Needs S to SW wind

Minimum wind: 15 kt at ridge height. Below 12 kt is usually too weak. Ridge soaring works year-round, even on winter cold-front days.

Wave Soaring

Wave lift forms downwind of mountains when stable air flows over them. Like water flowing over a rock.

Needs: 20+ kt wind perpendicular to ridge
Wind increasing with altitude
Stable layer near mountain-top height
Signs: lenticular clouds, rotor turbulence

At Worcester, NW flow across the Boland mountains produces wave. Entry is usually via ridge lift.

Cu Marking vs Blue Days

Cu (cumulus) clouds mark thermal tops. They're signposts showing where lift is.

Blue days have thermals but no clouds (too dry). You find lift by feel, terrain, and experience.

Cu-marked = Easier (fly toward developing cu)
Blue = Harder (use terrain triggers)
Spreadout/OD = Too much cu, sky fills in, kills thermals
Reading the RASP Sounding

The sounding (tephigram) shows the atmosphere's vertical profile:

Red line (right) = Temperature
Blue/green line (left) = Dewpoint
Gap between them = How dry the air is
Where they meet = Cloud forms (LCL)
Wind barbs (right side) = Wind at each level

Look for: Temp line leaning left steeply (unstable/good), lines close together (moisture/cu), kinks (inversions/caps), wind increasing with height (wave potential).
